# Shoot the shirts once, render designs in-house

Yes, InvAI can replace its code-drawn shirts with real photos of blank garments, but it should **not** hand each shop's design to an outside mockup service for every image. The best plan is a **hybrid**. InvAI gets a set of real blank-garment photos once (a house photoshoot, a shop's own photos, and later licensed stock if a license allows it). Each photo is prepared once, by a person or a tool, so the print area, folds and shadows are mapped. After that, InvAI's own `compose()` code in `invai-imaging` puts every design on those photos at scale. The outside tools fall short in three ways. Only one ready-made mockup API, **Dynamic Mockups**, was confirmed to accept your own photo-based Photoshop file (PSD), and its general Terms of Service **forbid commercial use** unless it gives separate written permission ([Dynamic Mockups ToS](https://dynamicmockups.com/terms-of-service/)). At its list price, a mid-size shop making 6,000 images a month would cost about **$310 a month**, against **about $6** of computing on InvAI's own servers. And sending every design to an outside service breaks decision 0022 §3, which says only `invai-imaging` may ever handle a design's pixels. For the base photos, the money question is the same one already open as OI-28. A studio charges **$39 per photo** ([Soona](https://soona.co/pricing)), so a lean core set of 30–45 photos costs about $1,300–1,900. A half-day custom shoot costs about $500–2,000. AI-made bases cost under $110, but every image built on them must carry an AI label. Much of the research behind this report is still unverified: web search ran out, so stock-license terms, the quality of AI-made bases, seller reviews and several vendors' terms could not be checked. The report ends with a short checklist that assigns each of those checks to an owner.

## Only one mockup API takes your own photos, and its terms block resale

The mockup market splits into two kinds of tool, and only one kind can make a photo look real. The first kind are **true smart-object tools**. A smart object is a replaceable layer in a Photoshop file; these tools warp the design along the fabric's folds and shade it with the photo's own light. Dynamic Mockups documents its template format as "Adobe Photoshop (.psd) with smart objects" and lets you add "your own unique mockups". It renders up to 4096×4096 px, at up to 300 requests a minute, and it **deletes rendered images after 24 hours** unless you are on the Enterprise plan ([Dynamic Mockups API](https://dynamicmockups.com/mockup-generator-api/)). Adobe's **Photoshop API** can "create and replace embedded and linked Smart Objects in a PSD", so InvAI could build its own photo templates and run them through Adobe's servers. It is a raw developer API, though, not a mockup product. InvAI would still have to build the template management itself, and its price page returned an error, so no per-call price was found ([Adobe Photoshop API](https://developer.adobe.com/firefly-services/docs/photoshop/)).

The second kind are **flat template tools**: Bannerbear at $49–299 a month ([Bannerbear](https://www.bannerbear.com/pricing/)), Placid ([Placid](https://placid.app/pricing)), Orshot ([Orshot](https://orshot.com/pricing)) and Mockuuups Studio at $0.10–0.20 per render ([Mockuuups](https://mockuuups.studio/api)). They paste an image into a fixed rectangle, the way social-media banners are made, with no folds and no fabric shading. That is the wrong category for garments, whatever the price. The free generators from Printful and Printify are tied to their own catalogs: Printful's API needs one of Printful's own product variant IDs ([Printful API](https://developers.printful.com/docs/)), and both use the platform's own product photos ([Printify](https://printify.com/mockup-generator/)). Placeit costs $7.47 a month but has **no API** ([Placeit](https://placeit.net/pricing)). The self-host options are thin as well. The Python library psd-tools explicitly **cannot edit smart objects** ([psd-tools](https://psd-tools.readthedocs.io/en/latest/)). Photopea's free scripting API is a hosted service that warns it "may have critical bugs" ([Photopea](https://www.photopea.com/api/)). Whether the JavaScript PSD library ag-psd can render smart objects is unknown.

The legal problem matters more than the technical one. Dynamic Mockups' pricing page sells a "White-label Product Customizer" on its Enterprise plan ([Dynamic Mockups pricing](https://www.dynamicmockups.com/pricing)). White-label means InvAI could offer the tool under its own name. But Section 7 of its general Terms of Service bans using the service "for any revenue-generating endeavor or commercial enterprise", and Section 2 limits content to "personal, non-commercial use or internal business purpose only". The terms also give **no retention period for uploaded files** ([Dynamic Mockups ToS](https://dynamicmockups.com/terms-of-service/)). A separate API or Enterprise agreement probably overrides this, but none was found. Until the vendor confirms in writing that InvAI may use it on behalf of many shops, Dynamic Mockups cannot be a production dependency. No other vendor's terms could be read during this research.

## Sending designs out costs money per image and breaks the design lock

The cost difference is structural. Dynamic Mockups charges 1 credit per standard render. Its Pro plan costs about $19 a month (or $15 a month billed yearly) and includes about 300 credits a month; extra credits cost **$0.051** each ([Dynamic Mockups pricing](https://dynamicmockups.com/pricing)). InvAI's own composite is CPU work only, estimated at about $0.001 per image. That figure is an estimate carried over from the earlier listing-images research, not a measurement. The table assumes small = 20 designs × 3 colors × 10 images, mid = 100 × 5 × 12, and large = 300 × 5 × 12, all **per shop, per month**.

| Monthly volume per shop | Dynamic Mockups (Pro + extra credits) | InvAI's own `compose()` (compute estimate) |
|---|---|---|
| Small: 600 renders | ≈ $34 | ≈ $0.60 |
| Mid: 6,000 renders | ≈ $310 ($291 in extra credits) | ≈ $6 |
| Large: 18,000 renders | ≈ $922 | ≈ $18 |

These figures are list-price arithmetic, not quotes. Enterprise pricing (5,000+ credits a month) is not published and could be much lower. Even so, vendor cost grows with every image for every shop: twenty mid-size shops would cost about $6,200 a month. InvAI's own cost grows only with servers, and the known server risk is memory. Peak usage of 570–700 MB per 16-template set is already logged as B-278.

Data exposure is the second cost. Decision 0022 §3 says "the design's pixels are only ever composited by `invai-imaging`, deterministically", and InvAI's AI-scene path follows it today: the outside image provider receives only a blank garment, never the design. A per-render mockup API, or Adobe's Photoshop API, would receive **every shop's unreleased artwork** and keep it for a period nobody has stated. That would be a new outside data processor (a sub-processor) for InvAI's privacy policy and data processing agreement (DPA), and a reversal of 0022 §3's core rule, not just its "never stock art" wording. The safe pattern is to send only blank photos out, if anything goes out at all, and composite the design inside InvAI.

## The hybrid reuses code InvAI already has

InvAI already has most of the mockup machinery. `photos.py` places the design at its true printed size with `_place()`, bends it along folds with `_shift()` using a height map (a grayscale map of where the fabric rises and sinks), and tints and shades it with `_garment_rgb()`. The only problem is the source: every map is hand-painted onto a garment that `garments.py` draws from curves, and the contract's `PhotoImageSource` allows only `template` or `ai_scene`. Supporting real photo bases needs three things that do not exist yet. First, a **print-area calibration** for each photo: where the printable rectangle sits, in real inches. Second, **fold and shading maps** derived from that photo. Third, an **occlusion mask**, which marks anything in front of the print (an arm, hair, hoodie drawstrings); B-287 shows that even today's drawn hoodies get this wrong. Doing all three automatically would need new computer-vision models that the codebase does not have. None of the competitors that could be checked offer it either: Dynamic Mockups' "custom template" is a PSD that a person prepares once, not something worked out from the photo's pixels.

So the hybrid copies what the market does, inside InvAI. Each base photo is calibrated once, by a designer in Photoshop or Photopea, or in an InvAI calibration screen (already planned in wave IMG1). The work is to set the print-area corner points, paint or derive the fold and shading maps, and mask any occlusion. The result is stored as a base bundle. From then on every design, color and marketplace crop is rendered by InvAI's own code. This is a **one-time cost for each photo, not a cost for each design**. Design files never leave InvAI. And it matches 0022 and ADR 0023, except for two amendments: §3's "drawn by code" wording, and 0023 §2's step that repaints the print area in the flat blank color.

| Option | Cost (S / M / L per shop per month) | Design and IP exposure | Fit with 0022 / ADR 0023 | Effort | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1. External mockup API per render** (Dynamic Mockups) | ≈ $34 / $310 / $922 | Every design goes to the vendor; upload retention unstated | **Breaks 0022 §3**; new sub-processor | Low to integrate | **Reject** for production; general ToS forbids commercial use |
| **2. Adobe Photoshop API per render** | Unknown (no public price) | Every design goes to Adobe | Breaks 0022 §3 | Medium–high (InvAI builds template management) | **Reject** for rendering; at most a one-time base-prep tool |
| **3. Own renderer, maps derived automatically** | ≈ $0.60 / $6 / $18 compute | None | Fits after the §3 wording amendment | **High**: segmentation, fold maps from photos and occlusion are all new | Later, only if manual calibration becomes the bottleneck |
| **4. Hybrid: calibrate each photo once, `compose()` at scale** | ≈ $0.60 / $6 / $18 compute, plus a one-time base cost | None (only blank photos ever leave, if at all) | Fits after amending §3 wording and 0023 §2 | **Medium**: base bundle, calibration screen, new `origin` field (the IMG1 wave) | **Recommend** |
| **5. Shops upload their own photos or PSDs** | Same as option 4 for photos | None for photos; PSDs would need options 1 or 2 to render | Photos fit as `shop_shoot`; PSDs don't (psd-tools can't render smart objects) | Low as an extension of option 4 | **Photos yes, in IMG1; PSD import no** |

## A lean shot list covers every main image

The base set only needs to cover the images where a real photo matters most: the main image on Amazon and Walmart, and Etsy's hero image. Three tees appear most often in best-selling Etsy mockups: **Comfort Colors 1717, Bella+Canvas 3001 and Gildan 5000**. They are usually shown folded or flat on a styled surface ([mockupmuse](https://mockupmuse.com/blogs/pod-growth-academy/best-tshirt-mockups-etsy)). Comfort Colors buyers have publicly complained that mockup tools show 1717 colors wrong ([MyDesigns community](https://community.mydesigns.io/c/general-discussions/comfort-colors-mockup-colors-are-wrong-please-double-check-your-listings)). So the plan is to photograph a handful of real colors and generate the rest by **recoloring**, rejecting any result whose color drifts too far from the official swatch (deltaE is the standard measure of how different two colors look). The official brand swatch values were not obtained in this research.

| Shot | Garments | Colors photographed | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Standing model, front, white background | 3 tees; 1 hoodie, 1 crewneck (blank models chosen by the PM from shop order data) | Top 3 per tee (e.g., white, black, one bestseller); 2 per fleece | Amazon's adult-apparel main image must show a real standing model; it is also the safest main image for Walmart |
| Standing model, back | Same | 1–2 per garment | Back prints; Amazon slot 2 |
| Flat lay or ghost mannequin, front, white | 3 tees + 2 fleece | Same as above | Main image for each color variant; source for the color grid and close-ups |
| Folded hero on a styled surface | 3 tees | 2 bestsellers | Etsy slot 1, the top seller pattern |
| Hanging (on a hanger or rail) | 1–2 tees | 1 | Optional variety for secondary slots |
| Lifestyle (worn outdoors or in a room) | 1–2 tees | 1–2 | Secondary slots; can be AI-made later |
| Close-up | none (a crop of a high-resolution flat or folded shot) | none | Print-quality slot |

That comes to roughly **30–45 frames**. The three ways to get them differ mainly in cost and in what each forces InvAI to disclose.

| Budget | How | Approx. cost | Disclosure consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **$0–110: AI-only bases** | An image model draws blank garments and models once, after OI-25 or OI-29 turns on a real provider; the design is never sent | About $50–110 for a full library (earlier estimate); quality **unverified** | Every image built on these bases needs Etsy's AI disclosure, Amazon's `contains-synthetic-performer` tag on photoreal people, and TikTok Shop's visible AI label. Not suitable as Amazon or Walmart main images |
| **About $1,300–1,900: per-photo studio** | Ship blanks to Soona: $39 per photo plus a $149 studio pass or $13–49 a month membership; ghost-mannequin styling $116–129 an hour ([Soona](https://soona.co/pricing)). Optional bulk retouching at $0.95 per image ([Pixelz](https://pixelz.com/pricing/)) | 35 photos ≈ $1,365 + $149, plus styling hours | None for real photos; check that the studio's model releases allow reuse by many shops |
| **About $500–2,000 plus extras: half-day custom shoot** | Local photographer, model(s) and stylist; plan the maps (even lighting, a straight-on angle) with the imaging engineer beforehand | $500–2,000 per half day per a 2026 cost survey, not quotes; model fees and releases on top | None; InvAI owns the full set, but the release must explicitly cover use across all tenants |

Per-photo studio pricing gets expensive past about 50 frames, while a half-day shoot gets more photos for the same money but takes more planning. Neither price was quoted for this specific job. A cheaper first step costs nothing: let pilot shops upload their own blank-garment photos into the hybrid pipeline, with origin `shop_shoot`. The shop must confirm it owns the photo and has a model release.

## Conclusion

The question has shifted from which tool renders the mockups to who prepares the photos. Rendering is the part InvAI already owns, does almost for free, and must keep in-house to honor its own design-lock decision. The one bought tool that fits does not sell well at InvAI's volume, and its terms do not yet permit InvAI's use. The real cost is one-time and human: shooting a few dozen frames and calibrating each one. That is a platform asset shared by every tenant, so it belongs in InvAI's budget rather than on each shop's bill.

For the open owner items: **OI-27** should be answered **A**, but in two steps. Allow house and shop photos now (option B's scope). Add licensed stock only after the compliance officer clears a license. The amendment should also state plainly that per-render external mockup APIs stay out, because they would break 0022 §3's core rule, not just its wording. **OI-28** should stay **A**, but sized by evidence: run the free imaging spike first, collect pilot shops' own photos at no cost, then fund a **core set of about 35 frames** (≈ $1,300–1,900 at Soona, or a quoted half-day shoot if cheaper). Do this only after a model release that covers multi-tenant use is ready. AI bases fill the lifestyle slots later, with disclosure.
